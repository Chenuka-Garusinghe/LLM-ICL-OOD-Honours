  Here's my honest read.

  TL;DR

  The headline story is not "SATA works" — it clearly doesn't transfer to the
  LLM — but what you have is a coherent and, I'd argue, genuinely interesting
  negative-result thesis with one striking mechanistic finding: SATA learns the
  intended selection policy almost perfectly by the proxy measure (Gate 2: 0.96
  XGBoost accuracy vs 0.72 for the best protocol), yet the same selections make
  the frozen LLM catastrophically worse — down to 5–10% accuracy on
  spurious-reversal, i.e. near-perfect anti-correlation with the truth. That
  dissociation between "demos that are good evidence for a learner" and "demos
  that are good prompt content for an LLM" is the most publishable thing in the
  results. But there are also two or three artifacts in the synthetic arm you
  need to fix or caveat before any of it is claimable.

  By research question

  RQ1 (does vanilla ICL degrade OOD?) — the premise mostly fails to reproduce.
  Across 3 datasets × 2 models, the ID−OOD shift gaps for few-shot methods are
  small and mostly negative (OOD ≥ ID). Only zero-shot Llama on ACS Public
  Coverage shows a big positive gap (+0.39), and that dataset is generally
  chaotic (Llama random-8 gets 0.337 on ID — below chance). The deeper issue is
  absolute performance: 0.5–0.73 accuracy nearly everywhere. TableShift's
  published gaps come from trained models (XGBoost etc.) that first reach high
  ID accuracy; a frozen 7–8B LLM with 8 serialized demos never acquires much
  competence to lose, so there's no room for a shift gap to appear. RQ1's honest
  answer is itself a finding: at this scale, tabular ICL is near the floor, and
  "OOD degradation" is masked by a competence ceiling. Say that plainly rather
  than hedging.

  RQ2 (protocol × shift interaction) — negative, and I think you can identify 
  the mechanism. The pre-registered success condition was a crossover
  (feature-range wins covariate, counter-spurious wins spurious-reversal).
  Instead, label diversity wins essentially every shift type on both models, and
  counter-spurious does not win spurious-reversal. The likely reason is sitting
  in your zero-shot data: the models predict class "1" 96–99% of the time
  regardless of environment. The dominant lever in this regime is the in-context
  label prior, and label diversity is the only protocol that explicitly
  controls it — which is exactly Min et al.'s claim (your ref [27]) that
  label-space statistics matter more than input–label mapping. So RQ2's result
  is "no shift-targeted interaction; demonstration effects at this model scale
  are dominated by label-balance correction." That's a real, well-grounded
  conclusion, and one cheap analysis would nail it: regress per-cell accuracy on
  the label balance of the selected demo set (you have demo_ids saved). I'd
  expect it to explain most of the between-protocol variance.

  RQ3 (accuracy–faithfulness dissociation) — technically satisfied, practically 
  underpowered. The scatter shows no relationship between Δaccuracy and Δρ, and
  real-arm ρ values are small (−0.19 to 0.41) with bootstrap CIs straddling zero
  almost everywhere; the synthetic per-task ρ values are mostly non-significant
  with some NaNs. You can honestly say faithfulness is low everywhere and
  uncorrelated with accuracy gains — consistent with the STaDS finding you built
  on — but a Spearman over ~10 features has enormous sampling variance, so
  frame this descriptively ("no evidence that accuracy gains buy faithfulness")
  rather than as a demonstrated dissociation.

  RQ4 (SATA) — a strong negative result with an actual story. Three facts line
  up:

  1. SATA did learn the target policy: KL loss falls cleanly, proxy validation
  0.92–0.96 throughout training.
  2. With the LLM, SATA variants are the worst methods everywhere, and on
  spurious-reversal they hit 0.05–0.10 accuracy. Predictions there are balanced
  (~50/50), labels are balanced, yet agreement is 6% — the model is disagreeing
  with the truth ~94% of the time, which is almost exactly what you'd get if the
  LLM is reading the spurious feature positively in an environment where it
  agrees with the label only ~10% of the time. In other words, SATA's selections
  don't just fail — they induce near-total shortcut reliance. That's arguably
  the cleanest measured instance in your whole thesis of "demonstrations
  redirect feature reliance" — the central question from the lit review — just
  in the wrong direction.
  3. The query-agnostic ablation beats full SATA (Qwen: 0.56 vs 0.045 on
  spurious-reversal), the exact opposite of the design hypothesis. Per-query
  conditioning is what breaks it, plausibly because query-conditioned selection
  (same-regime + label-match bonuses in the target scores) produces demo sets
  with skewed label/feature statistics, and the LLM consumes those statistics
  shallowly rather than inferring the rule.

  Two follow-up computations would turn this from "weird numbers" into a
  defensible mechanism, and both are cheap since you have demo_ids and the
  frozen task data: (a) the counter-spurious fraction and label balance of
  SATA-selected demo sets per environment, and (b) a direct "spurious agreement
  rate" P(prediction = spurious-feature-implied label) per method × environment
  — for SATA on reversal I'd expect ~0.9+.

  Artifacts you need to fix or caveat

  - The held-out-family missing_feature environment is degenerate: all 1600
  labels are 0 (sparse-interaction rule is a product — zero one causal factor
  and y is constant), so label_diversity's 0.000 accuracy there is just the
  model's "1"-bias meeting a constant-label set. Drop that cell or regenerate;
  don't let a 0.000 sit in a thesis table unexplained.
  - The covariate environment leaks label shift: its base rate is 0.388 vs ~0.49
  elsewhere, because shifting feature means moves P(y) too. That confound,
  combined with the models' "1"-bias, fully explains why "covariate" looks like
  the hardest shift for every method. Either resample to hold the base rate
  fixed or lean on macro-F1 and say so explicitly — otherwise your covariate
  column measures label shift, which undermines the taxonomy argument your lit
  review makes a point of.
  - Report the prediction-bias diagnostic (the 96–99% class-1 rate) prominently
  — it's load-bearing for interpreting both arms, and a contextual-calibration
  baseline would show how much of the protocol differences it accounts for.

  How I'd frame the thesis

  Your spec pre-authorized this pivot ("if SATA doesn't beat the best protocol,
  RQ4 becomes a rigorous negative result"), and the pieces assemble well: (1)
  frozen mid-size LLMs are near-floor on tabular ICL, so the OOD-degradation
  premise doesn't manifest; (2) in this regime demonstration design mostly
  operates through in-context label statistics, not shift-targeted structure;
  (3) a meta-learned selector that provably learns the "right" policy backfires
  because LLMs don't use demonstrations the way a learner uses training data —
  and the failure is predictable and measurable (the spurious-reversal collapse,
  the query-agnostic ablation winning). That last point speaks directly to the
  TabPFN-vs-general-LLM distinction your reviewer comments in the lit review
  were probing. It's a more honest and more interesting thesis than a marginal
  positive result would have been — the main work remaining is cleaning up the
  two synthetic-arm artifacts and adding the demo-composition analyses so the
  mechanism claims are backed by direct measurement rather than inference from
  accuracy patterns.

⏺ Both code explorations are complete, and the mechanism behind the failures is
  now fully explained — this redesign has very solid grounding. The most
  important discoveries:

  1. SATA's target function leaks the query's true label (+0.5 label-match
  bonus), and the spurious feature is literally the label ±noise at 96–99.5%
  strength. So SATA learned exactly the shortcut it was meant to fight: "read
  the query's label off feature 8, pick demos with that label." Gate 2's 0.96
  proxy accuracy ≈ the spurious strength — the smoking gun. In
  spurious-reversal, feature 8 anti-predicts the label, so SATA picks
  wrong-label demos, the LLM copies the majority demo label, and accuracy
  collapses to 5–10%. Fully deterministic, fully fixable.
  2. The instruct LLMs are run without their chat templates (raw completion
  mode), with semantically empty prompts (feature_0: -0.43; ...), no
  calibration, and the models predict "1" 96–99% of the time zero-shot. That's
  why everything sits near chance.
  3. Generator bugs: sparse-interaction's threshold is never sampled (stays
  0.0), so the held-out-family × missing-feature cell is ~98% constant-label;
  covariate shift moves the label base rate; extrapolation is label-preserving
  for two rule families and erased by standardisation; demo pools always come
  from the shifted test environment (an oracle assumption).
  4. Training pathology: SATA's "best" checkpoint is epoch 0, and the proxy
  metric falls as KL loss improves — the target and the goal actively disagree.

⏺ Short answer: yes, but narrowly and for a specific reason — and the lit review
  itself names the candidates.

  What the lit review says. Section 2.1.3 covers three tabular-shift benchmarks,
  and your review explicitly frames them as complementary: TableShift [6] for
  broad coverage of covariate/label/concept shift, WHYSHIFT (Liu et al. [7])
  which "isolates the harder concept-shift regime" — 22 settings built from
  Taxi, US Accident, and ACS data where the input–output mapping itself moves —
  and STaDS [8], which is less a dataset source than the faithfulness-evaluation
  protocol you already adopted (π_self vs π_behav is taken from it). So the
  review positions WHYSHIFT as the piece the real arm currently doesn't use.

  Why this matters for your specific situation. The v1 real-arm finding was that
  TableShift's natural shifts produced no gap for ICL — partly the competence
  floor, but plausibly also because TableShift's headline shifts
  (geographic/temporal on ACS-style data) are relatively mild covariate shifts.
  Concept shift — P(y|x) moving — is the regime your own lit review calls
  hardest to correct and where shortcut reliance should actually be punished.
  That's exactly what WHYSHIFT settings are constructed to isolate, and it's
  where your rule-diversity and counter-spurious protocols have their
  theoretical bite. If real-data OOD degradation exists anywhere for ICL, it's
  most likely there. There's a second alignment: your methodology section
  already commits to the Liu et al. shift-attribution decomposition (ΔX vs ΔY|X)
  for RQ2 — that procedure comes from the WHYSHIFT paper, so adding one or two
  of its settings gives you data the decomposition was designed for.

  What I'd recommend against. Don't add breadth for its own sake. The synthetic
  arm exists precisely because real datasets can't isolate shift types —
  diversifying real data buys external validity, not mechanism. And each dataset
  multiplies the grid: the real-arm re-run is already ~24–48 GPU-hours, and
  every addition also needs preprocessing, feature reduction, and a demo pool.
  STaDS's 15 datasets I'd skip entirely — you're already using its protocol,
  which is the part that matters.

  Concrete suggestion: keep the current TableShift datasets as the core, and add
  1–2 WHYSHIFT concept-shift settings (e.g. a Taxi setting and one of its ACS
  Income concept-shift variants) — but only after Gate S1 passes. If the fixed
  inference layer still leaves the models near the competence floor, extra
  datasets just replicate the null more expensively; if Gate S1 passes, the
  WHYSHIFT additions become the strongest real-data test of the whole
  hypothesis. I can add this as a conditional Stage-4 item in the plan and a
  paragraph in the rationale doc if you want.

  measured on trained models like xgboost and friends but they werer allowed to

⏺ This is the "competence floor" (or ceiling) argument, and it's worth getting
  precise because it determines why Gate S1 exists and why RQ1 came back null.
  Let me build it up.

  What a shift gap actually measures. TableShift's benchmark numbers come from
  models like XGBoost trained on the full training split — hundreds of thousands
  of rows for the ACS datasets. Those models reach high ID accuracy (often
  0.85+) because they've genuinely learned the mapping from features to label on
  the source distribution. The shift gap (ID accuracy − OOD accuracy) then
  measures how much of what was learned fails to transfer: the model encoded
  patterns that were true in the training distribution, some of those patterns
  are distribution-specific (the shortcuts), and when the distribution moves,
  the accuracy attributable to those patterns evaporates. The gap is literally
  the measurement of losing something you had.

  A gap requires having something to lose. Decompose any classifier's accuracy
  informally as: baseline (what you'd get from the label prior alone —
  majority-class guessing) plus learned signal (what the model extracts from the
  features). Distribution shift attacks the learned-signal part — it can't take
  away what guessing gives you, because guessing doesn't depend on the feature
  distribution at all. So the maximum observable gap is bounded by roughly
  ID_accuracy − baseline. An XGBoost sitting at 0.90 ID with a 0.60 baseline has
  30 points of learned signal exposed to the shift; a gap of 10 points is
  easily visible. A model sitting at 0.55 ID with a 0.50 baseline has 5 points
  of learned signal — even if the shift destroyed all of it, the measured gap
  would be 5 points, comparable to seed noise.

  Our frozen LLMs are in the second regime. They never see the training split —
  they see 8 serialised rows in a prompt. On the synthetic arm the numbers make
  this stark: XGBoost trained on the same 64-demo pools clears 0.80 ID, while
  most LLM conditions sit at ~0.50 ID — i.e., the LLM extracted essentially zero
  learned signal from the demonstrations. Its behaviour is dominated by its
  prior (the 96–99% class-1 bias) plus the label statistics of the demos.
  Neither of those depends on whether the test row came from the ID or the OOD
  distribution, so ID and OOD scores land in the same place and the "gap" is
  noise around zero. The analogy: a student who scores 95% on the practice exam
  and 60% on the altered exam has revealed that the alteration matters; a
  student guessing ~50% on both has revealed nothing about the alteration — only
  that they can't do the exam.

  It also explains the weird negative gaps. When accuracy is mostly "prior bias
  meets base rate," whichever split's label base rate happens to align with the
  model's bias scores higher — and that's arbitrary. You can see it in our
  real-arm data: on ACS Public Coverage, zero-shot Llama scores 0.794 ID vs
  0.402 OOD (huge apparent gap), while zero-shot Qwen on the same dataset scores
  0.254 ID vs 0.604 OOD (huge gap in the opposite direction). No robustness
  story explains both; base-rate alignment explains both trivially. Same
  mechanism behind Llama random-8 scoring 0.337 (below chance) on ID there. None
  of these numbers is measuring generalisation.

  The subtle point: this doesn't mean TableShift's gaps are wrong, or that ours 
  "should" match. Both measurements are correct; they're measuring different
  objects. Theirs: how much a fitted model's learned mapping survives shift.
  Ours: how a frozen model's prior-plus-prompt behaviour varies across splits.
  The v1 mistake was assuming the published gaps would reappear in our setting —
  the lit review's RQ1 framing implicitly assumed the LLM would first reach
  enough ID competence for degradation to have room to show up. At 7–8B with an
  unformatted prompt, it didn't.

  Which is exactly why the redesign gates on it. Gate S1 requires random-8 to
  hit ≥ 0.60 ID accuracy (against a 0.50 baseline) after the inference-layer
  fixes — that's a demand for ~10+ points of learned signal, the minimum
  headroom for OOD degradation to be measurable at all. If 7–8B can't clear it,
  we escalate model size, because RQ1–RQ4 are all conditional on there being
  some competence whose robustness we can interrogate. You can't study whether
  something breaks under shift until it exists.

  compressed into two sentences in §3/RQ1)?

❯ what is meant by proxy accuracy in @results/sata_gate2_summary.parquet        
  ⎿  Read results/sata_gate2_summary.parquet (20 lines)

⏺ "Proxy accuracy" is a stand-in metric for "does this demo-selection method
  pick good demonstrations?" that avoids running the LLM at all — hence "proxy."
  It's computed by evaluate_sata_proxy (and NB05's equivalent for the
  protocols) like this, for each method:

  1. Take a validation task, generate its id environment only (64 demos, 32
  queries).
  2. For each query, have the method select its top-k=8 demos from the pool
  (SATA scores and ranks them; the protocols use their own selection rule).
  3. Fit a small XGBoost classifier on just those 8 selected demos (features +
  labels).
  4. Have that XGBoost predict the query's label. Score 1 if correct, 0 if not.
  5. Average over all queries across all validation tasks → proxy_accuracy for
  that method.

  So it's asking: "if I handed only this method's chosen 8 rows to a generic 
  learner, could that learner classify the held-out query correctly?" It's a
  cheap way to compare demo-selection quality without burning GPU time on the
  actual LLM — this is what Gate 2 in the original spec was built around (SATA
  should beat the best protocol here before spending compute on the real LLM
  evaluation).

  The values in your file:

  ┌──────────────────┬────────────────┐
  │      method      │ proxy_accuracy │
  ├──────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ sata             │ 0.961          │
  ├──────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ label_diversity  │ 0.724          │
  ├──────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ rule_diversity   │ 0.617          │
  ├──────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ random           │ 0.562          │
  ├──────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ counter_spurious │ 0.544          │
  └──────────────────┴────────────────┘

  On its face, SATA looks like it dominates — that's what made Gate 2 "pass" and
  greenlit training/deployment.

  Why this number is misleading, per the diagnosis we did earlier: two things in
  the measurement let a shortcut pass as a win.

  1. It only evaluates on the id environment — never on spurious_reversal,
  mechanism, etc. — so it can't detect a method that only works by exploiting a
  feature that happens to correlate with the label in id.
  2. There's a fallback in _fit_predict_one: if the selected 8 demos are all the
  same label, skip fitting XGBoost entirely and just predict that label.
  Because SATA's target function had a term rewarding demos whose label matches
  the query's true label, and because the spurious feature (feature 8) predicts
  that label at 96–99.5% strength, SATA converged on a policy of "infer the
  query's label from feature 8, then select 8 demos carrying that label." That
  produces near-single-class selections, which hit the fallback, which is just a
  direct (accurate) label guess — not a demonstration of good selection at all.
  0.961 is essentially the spurious-feature strength, not a measure of 
  selection quality.

  That's why this same method collapses to ~0.05–0.10 accuracy once you actually
  run it through the LLM under spurious_reversal (where the shortcut points the
  wrong way) — the proxy accuracy and the real downstream result disagree by
  ~0.9, which is the central piece of evidence in the redesign rationale. The v2
  plan's Gate S3a/S3b fixes both defects: evaluate the proxy across all 6
  environments (worst-case, not just id), and remove the label-leaking target
  term so a method can't win the proxy by reconstructing the label instead of
  learning structure.

❯ is the validation proxy for sata based off of a XGBoost proxy ? -- and also   
  oculd you quickly explain val proxy                                           

⏺ Yes — same mechanism, same evaluate_sata_proxy function, just used at a
  different point.

  val_proxy (the column in sata_training_log.parquet) is that XGBoost-proxy
  accuracy computed once per training epoch, on the validation tasks, using
  SATA's current (mid-training) weights to select the top-k demos. So each
  epoch:

  1. SATA (current checkpoint) scores and ranks demos for each validation query
  on the id environment.
  2. Top-8 selected demos → fit XGBoost → predict the query → accuracy.
  3. Average across validation queries/tasks → that epoch's val_proxy.

  It's used for two things in train_sata:
  - Checkpointing — whichever epoch has the highest val_proxy gets saved as
  sata_best.pt.
  - Early stopping — training halts if val_proxy hasn't improved in patience
  (10) epochs.

  How it relates to proxy_accuracy in sata_gate2_summary.parquet: that file is
  essentially the final snapshot of this same computation — val_proxy at the
  best checkpoint for SATA, reported side-by-side with the same XGBoost-proxy
  metric computed for the other selection methods (random, label_diversity,
  etc.), so Gate 2 could compare them on equal footing. So sata's row in the
  gate-2 summary (0.961) is the peak val_proxy value from the training log.

  That peak, per the training log we pulled earlier, occurred at epoch 0 —
  val_proxy fell monotonically afterward (0.958 → 0.924) even as the KL training
  loss kept improving (0.298 → 0.184). That's the anomaly flagged in the
  diagnosis: the thing being optimised (KL loss against the target function) and
  the thing being used to pick the checkpoint (val_proxy) moved in opposite
  directions almost immediately — a sign the target function was training SATA
  toward something that looks worse by this proxy the more it's learned, which
  lines up with our reading that continued training pushed it further into the

  1. Majority-label copying (no attention to feature 8 required). Because SATA

  has to look at feature 8 at all — it just parrots whichever label dominates
  the demos, and that label is wrong under reversal because SATA inferred it

  feature 8 sign matches its label (that's true in every environment, just with
  flipped polarity under reversal), the LLM could be doing exactly what ICL
  theory says it does — inferring "the rule connecting demos to labels" from the
  context (Xie et al.'s Bayesian view [14]) — and the rule it infers,
  correctly, is "label = sign(feature_8)". It then applies that
  correctly-inferred-but-wrong-for-this-task rule to the query's own feature 8
  value.

  shouldn't have implied it was settled. Both produce the same aggregate

  and that class label is derived from feature 8, so "copy the demo majority"
  and "apply the feature-8 rule to the query" give the same prediction almost
  every time. The aggregate accuracy (≈ 1 − spurious_strength) is consistent

⏺ "Fact B" is about why the label-leak in SATA's target function (Fact A — the
  +0.5 bonus for demo.label == query.label) turned into a dominant shortcut
  rather than a harmless, mild nudge. The reason is entirely in how feature 8 is
  constructed.

  The construction, from generator.py:

  agree = rng.random(n_samples) < spurious_strength
  X[:, self.spurious_idx] = np.where(agree, y_clean, 1 - y_clean).astype(float)
  \
                            + rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=n_samples)

  Read this literally: feature 8's value is the label itself (0 or 1), with
  probability agree (≈ 96–99.5%, since spurious_strength_range: [0.96, 0.995] in
  the config), or the flipped label the rest of the time — and then a small
  Gaussian wobble of standard deviation 0.1 is added on top.

  Why "near-photocopy" is the right word, not hyperbole. A photocopy of a
  document isn't the original — there's toner smudging, slight blurring — but
  you can still read every word off it without needing to reconstruct the
  document from context. That's exactly the relationship between feature 8 and
  the label: take the label (a clean 0 or 1), copy it into feature 8, and blur
  it with noise of std 0.1. Since the two possible "clean" values (0 and 1) are
  1.0 apart and the blur is only 0.1 wide, the blur essentially never crosses
  the midpoint. So the label is recoverable from feature 8 by the simplest
  possible operation — round to the nearest integer, or equivalently threshold
  at 0.5 — and that single-threshold rule is correct precisely as often as agree
  is true: 96–99.5% of the time.

  "Trivially reconstructable" is a contrast with what the task is actually 
  supposed to require. The real task — the thing SATA and the LLM are supposed
  to be learning to do — is to infer the label from the causal features via one
  of four rule families (linear combination, threshold logic, a decision tree,
  or a feature-product interaction), using only 64 examples, without knowing in
  advance which features are causal or what the rule's parameters are. That's a
  genuine induction problem. Reading feature 8 and rounding it is not an
  induction problem at all — it requires looking at one column and applying one
  comparison. "Trivially reconstructable" means: you don't need to solve the
  task to get the label right 96–99.5% of the time: you need to read one number.

  Why this specifically matters for the target function's "mild" label bonus.
  The code comment says the intent was for this term to be mild:

  # Correct label bonus (mild)
  # Not too strong — we want structural alignment, not just label matching
  if demo["label"] == query_metadata["label"]:
      score += 0.5

  +0.5 is indeed smaller than the +2.0 same-regime bonus and the +1.5
  counter-spurious bonus — on paper, label-matching should be a minor
  tiebreaker. But "mild weight" only stays mild if the term is hard to satisfy 
  well. If satisfying it required actually knowing the query's true label —
  which SATA cannot observe directly — the term would contribute noise, not a
  learnable signal, and the model would fall back on the two structural terms it
  can learn (regime and counter-spurious status, both computable from features
  it has). That was presumably the design assumption.

  But because feature 8 makes the query's label nearly free to infer, the
  label-match term stops being "hard to satisfy" and becomes the single easiest
  term in the entire loss to drive toward zero — easier than learning what a
  "regime" even is (a sign-pattern over 3–5 causal features, i.e. actual
  structure), and far easier than learning the sparse, environment-dependent
  counter-spurious signal. SATA is a 4-layer transformer that receives every
  demo's label as an explicit input and the query's features (including feature
  8) as another input — learning "threshold the query's feature 8, then upweight
  demos whose given label matches that threshold" is a one-feature,
  one-comparison rule, trivially within reach of that architecture in a handful
  of gradient steps. That matches what the training log shows: SATA's
  proxy-validation score was already at 0.958 at epoch 0 — consistent with
  grabbing the cheap one-feature shortcut almost immediately, rather than
  gradually learning a harder multi-feature structural rule over many epochs.

  So Fact B is the enabling condition for Fact A: the label-match term in the
  target function is only a real design mistake because feature 8 happens to
  make it exploitable almost for free. Remove Fact B (e.g. lower
  spurious_strength so feature 8 is a weaker, breakable correlate rather than a
  near-copy) and the label-match term would still be a design smell, but nowhere
  near as damaging. Remove Fact A (the label-match term itself) and Fact B
  stops mattering to SATA's training, because nothing in the target function
  would reward inferring the query's label anymore — which is exactly the v2 fix
  (§5.3 in the rationale doc: the target function is rewritten with the
  label-match term deleted).
